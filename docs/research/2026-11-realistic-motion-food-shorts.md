# Fix the still, then stage contact

**Your bench shows two separate problems, and they need different fixes.** The food looks fake because of the first frame: you scored LTX 2.3's fidelity to the still 4-5/5 but food realism 1/5 on two of three clips, so the animation carried a weak still faithfully. The first fix is a better stills stage. Generate at 1-2 MP instead of 384x640, with a stronger open image model than FLUX.2 klein 4B (or a real stock photo), and use editorial-photography prompts. The patty flip looked fake because flips, tosses, cuts and pours are the action class every 2026 video model still fails. A free change on the Mac will not turn LTX 2.3 into a physics engine. What does work locally is constraining the action. Pin the before and after contact states with ltx-2-mlx multi-image anchors. Drive the motion from a phone clip of your own hand through LTX-2.3's Union Control IC-LoRA. Render 4-8 seeds and keep the best. Where the action still fails, redesign the shot so no single clip has to show the whole state change, and let foley sell the contact. Your blind test, where neither LTX-2.5 nor Wan 2.2 5B beat LTX 2.3, matches the benchmarks: no open model has a contact-physics lead big enough to justify switching. Paid models such as Seedance 2.5 rank higher but still fail physics most of the time, so keep them for a few hero shots, and only after you approve the spend. Several of the strongest recommendations below (anchor recipes, IC-LoRA on a q4 pack, Prompt Relay for actions) are documented features whose effect on contact has never been measured. The test plan at the end exists to measure them on your own dishes.

## The 384x640 still caps realism before LTX starts

Lightricks states plainly that the reference image's **"image quality, framing, and lighting… directly affect output quality across all frames,"** and that the input should **match or exceed the target output resolution** ([LTX blog](https://ltx.io/blog/image-to-video-workflow)). Your still is 384x640 and your output is 704x1280, so LTX gets roughly a quarter of the pixels it renders. That leaves little room for crumb, crust, oil beads and grill-mark micro-texture, which is a plausible cause of the smooth "AI food" look (inference). The same guide says two-stage pipelines need dimensions divisible by 64 and re-apply the reference at full strength in stage 2 to keep fine detail ([LTX blog](https://ltx.io/blog/image-to-video-workflow)). A 1088x1920 or 768x1344 still, downscaled to the working size, fits both rules. A realism-LoRA author makes the related point that detail enhancers **"enhance existing detail but don't create it"** and that inputs under 512 px work poorly ([Civitai](https://civitai.com/articles/27526/flux2-klein-9b-realistic-enhanced-details-lora)). Upscaling your current 384x640 output with SeedVR2, which mflux ships locally ([mflux](https://github.com/filipstrand/mflux)), is the fallback rather than the fix, because generative upscalers invent texture.

The model is the second limit. On the Artificial Analysis open-weights text-to-image arena, **FLUX.2 klein 4B sits at Elo 840 (#29)**. Mac-runnable alternatives score much higher: **Z-Image Turbo at 936** and **FLUX 3 Image Klein at 1040**. Qwen-Image-2.1 tops the board at 1047 ([AA open-weights leaderboard](https://artificialanalysis.ai/image/leaderboard/text-to-image/open-weights)). For a monetised channel, licence decides between them. Z-Image Turbo is Apache-2.0, 6B, 8 steps and "fits comfortably within 16G VRAM" ([HF card](https://huggingface.co/Tongyi-MAI/Z-Image-Turbo)), with an MLX build ([mlx-community](https://huggingface.co/mlx-community/Z-Image-Turbo-bf16)). FLUX 3 Image Klein (5.6B, Oct 2026) is free for commercial use under $5M annual revenue, supports editing and up to 10 reference images, and is built for LoRA training ([BFL](https://bfl.ai/blog/flux-3-image-klein)). Both run in mflux and Draw Things ([mflux](https://github.com/filipstrand/mflux); [Draw Things](https://apps.apple.com/app/draw-things/id6444050820)). Qwen-Image-2.1 ships under a **Research licence** ([Qwen README](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/QwenLM/Qwen-Image-2.1/main/README.md)), and FLUX.2 klein 9B is non-commercial ([eachlabs](https://www.eachlabs.ai/blog/flux-2-klein-4b-vs-9b)), so avoid both for published output. One caveat matters: the arena measures general human preference, not food. No source measured any of these on food, and nobody has published seconds-per-image figures on M-series chips.

The cheapest diagnostic is to skip generation entirely. Pexels photos are **free for commercial use and modification, with no attribution required**. The licence bans implied endorsement, resale of unaltered copies and trademark use ([Pexels licence](https://www.pexels.com/license/)), and it says nothing about AI use. Feed a real dish photo, cropped to 9:16 at 1080x1920, into your existing LTX path. If food realism jumps from 1 to 4-5, you have confirmed the bottleneck and gained a fallback for dishes where a real photo exists. A self-shot photo is cleaner still on rights.

Prompting is the free third lever. Food-photography guides agree that empty prompt slots fall back to the "flat overhead on white" AI default. They recommend naming the light type and direction, the lens and aperture, and the angle: 45° by default, eye level for burgers and steaks. They also recommend naming micro-textures such as "crust char, condensation droplets, specular highlights on… oil" and generating 3-5 variants per dish ([Ropewalk](https://ropewalk.ai/blog/ai-food-photography-guide-2026)). These sources are vendor blogs with no data behind them. General AI tells include over-smooth surfaces, perfect symmetry, glowing light with mismatched shadows, and over-saturation. The fixes are subtle imperfections, shallow depth of field, mild grain and muted colour ([Cloudinary](https://cloudinary.com/guides/image-generation/how-to-make-ai-images-look-real)). In practice that means "hand-formed, uneven patty, patchy Maillard browning, grease beading only at the edge, worn walnut board, crumbs, 100mm macro f/2.8, single warm key light from left with bounce" in place of "hyper-detailed 8K" (inference). Z-Image Turbo and Krea 2 Turbo run at guidance 0 ([HF card](https://huggingface.co/Tongyi-MAI/Z-Image-Turbo); [krea-2 README](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/krea-ai/krea-2/main/README.md)), so on those models negative prompts likely do nothing and the positive prompt has to carry everything.

## Flips fail because momentum and contact are the hardest physics class

Your finding that lowering and dropping looked fine while the flip looked like AI matches the literature closely. Diffusion video models have **"no skeleton, collision mesh, or joint constraints,"** so pixels at a touch boundary get averaged into merging and texture bleed ([invideo FAQ](https://invideo.io/faq/why-do-ai-video-models-struggle-with-physical-contact/)). On VideoPhy-2, **conservation of mass and momentum were the most-violated laws (about 40%)**, while reflection and buoyancy stayed under 20%. The hard subset, which includes tossing, pouring to overflow and breaking, topped out at **21.9%** for the best model ([VideoPhy-2](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2503.06800)). A flip is a momentum-plus-rotation-plus-contact event. Lowering is a slow motion along gravity that the model has seen many times. Even the November 2026 frontier is weak here: PhyRubric-Bench puts **Seedance 2.5 at 44.2% joint physics performance, Veo 3.1 at 36.2% and the best open model at 14.9%** ([PhyRubric](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.13576)). LTX does not appear on the Physics-IQ or VideoPhy-2 human leaderboards. Wan 2.2 5B scores 27.7 on Physics-IQ Verified i2v, below Wan 2.2 14B at 32.2 and Cosmos3-Super at 42.7 ([Physics-IQ](https://github.com/google-deepmind/physics-IQ-benchmark)). That is consistent with your blind test and argues against switching base models for physics.

Your observation that text-only clips were worse also has support. Professional AI studios lock a keyframe first (four options, pick one) and then animate it. invideo reports **about 3 generations per usable shot, with over 40% of final shots stitched from two or more generations**, and says prompt rewriting alone failed on contact shots ([invideo FAQ](https://invideo.io/faq/why-do-ai-video-models-struggle-with-physical-contact/)). Keep the still-first gate. It is the right architecture.

## Five local levers for contact, from cheapest to strongest

All flags below come from ltx-2-mlx at HEAD 747b30e, docs "current as of v0.16.3". The raw README on the web is stale. It still lists the removed `keyframe` command. So run `ltx-2-mlx generate --help` first to confirm that your installed version has `--image … replace|guide`, `--segment` and `--nag`.

**Lever 1: rewrite the action as chronological physical beats and time-gate them.** Lightricks' guide says to give **"each sentence a verb that does something,"** to describe events start to finish in present tense, and to write a single continuous take for I2V. It warns that the duration predictor **"won't stretch a moment or add a pause you didn't prompt for,"** and that **"simpler, more plausible motion is more reliable"** ([LTX prompting guide](https://docs.ltx.io/open-source-model/usage-guides/prompting-guide)). ltx-2-mlx adds Prompt Relay, `--segment "text" [LEN]`, which uses a training-free Gaussian penalty to gate each sub-prompt to a slice of the timeline. It works in all generate modes and is validated on 2.3 ([ltx-2-mlx README](https://github.com/dgrauet/ltx-2-mlx)). For a flip, split the prompt into approach, slide-under, lift-and-turn, land and withdraw segments. Write one sentence for what stays still ("the grill and bun do not move") and give audio its own sentence. That costs nothing, but no source has evaluated Prompt Relay on contact actions. The notes also do not state the unit of `LEN`, so check `--help`.

**Lever 2: anchor the contact states, not the mid-air pose.** The documented syntax is `--image PATH FRAME STRENGTH [auto|replace|guide] [CRF]`. It is repeatable, `FRAME` can be `last` or negative, and it works on every generate mode and on `ic-lora` ([ltx-2-mlx README](https://github.com/dgrauet/ltx-2-mlx)). `replace` hard-replaces a latent but **only at pixel frame 0 or 1+8k**. `guide` adds soft reference tokens at any frame ([CLAUDE.md](https://github.com/dgrauet/ltx-2-mlx/blob/main/CLAUDE.md)). The strength gap is measurable: a `replace` anchor held about 24-26 dB PSNR over the following frames, while a guide peaked at 26.7 dB and fell to 20.5 dB within eight frames ([CLAUDE.md](https://github.com/dgrauet/ltx-2-mlx/blob/main/CLAUDE.md)). The repo also warns that a pose the motion **"cannot plausibly reach is blended rather than reached"** ([PIPELINES.md](https://github.com/dgrauet/ltx-2-mlx/blob/main/docs/PIPELINES.md)). So anchor only stable states: the spatula under the patty at frame 0, and the patty seared side up with the spatula withdrawn near the end. Never anchor a patty frozen mid-rotation, which would come out as morphing (inference). In a 121-frame clip, the last valid `replace` slot is **frame 113 (1+8·14)**; frame 120 can only be a guide. Make the end-state image with an image-edit pass on the frame-0 still, for example FLUX 3 Klein's bounding-box-confined edits ([BFL](https://bfl.ai/blog/flux-3-image-klein)). That keeps lighting and props identical, because a mismatch gets blended into melting (inference). One more 2.3-specific detail: anchors are re-compressed at **CRF 33 by default on 2.3 packs**, and `0` disables re-compression ([PIPELINES.md](https://github.com/dgrauet/ltx-2-mlx/blob/main/docs/PIPELINES.md)). That likely softens grill marks and knife edges, so pass CRF 0 on both anchors. A composed, untested command looks like this:

```
ltx-2-mlx generate --distilled -H 1280 -W 704 -f 121 --seed S \
  --image still_contact.png 0 1.0 replace 0 \
  --image still_flipped.png 113 1.0 replace 0 \
  --segment "The spatula slides under the patty's edge." … \
  -p "<4-8 sentence chronological prompt>" -o flip_S.mp4
```

Note what the repo itself says about limits. Generate modes **"don't preserve the input image's identity over 4 sec with descriptive prompts — even with multi-anchor I2V,"** because the model treats anchors as initialisation ([CLAUDE.md](https://github.com/dgrauet/ltx-2-mlx/blob/main/CLAUDE.md)). `--num-generated-keyframes`, which relaxes temporal compression on fast motion, is **refused on 2.3 packs** ([PIPELINES.md](https://github.com/dgrauet/ltx-2-mlx/blob/main/docs/PIPELINES.md)).

**Lever 3: shorten the clip to the action.** Your 121 frames at 24 fps are about 5 s. A single contact action needs about 2-3 s, or 49-73 frames on the 8k+1 grid ([ltx-2-mlx README](https://github.com/dgrauet/ltx-2-mlx)), and spare seconds invite the unprompted drift the repo describes. Kling's guide likewise recommends 3-6 s clips for motion consistency ([fal Kling guide](https://fal.ai/learn/tools/how-to-use-kling-3-0-pro)). This is an inference from guidance; no study shows frame count changing contact accuracy. It also makes Lever 4 cheaper.

**Lever 4: render N seeds and select, or re-roll only the contact window.** Best-of-N has the best evidence of any physics technique, though not on LTX. On Physics-IQ, best-of-N with a world-model reward lifted Wan 2.2 from **38.3% to 44.4%**, and the leading entries all use best-of-8 ([Physics-IQ](https://github.com/google-deepmind/physics-IQ-benchmark); [arXiv 2601.10553](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.10553)). Video-T1 found VBench gains of +1.6% to +5.9% as N grew to 30, levelling off toward an upper limit ([Video-T1](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.18942v2)). On distilled q4, 4-8 seeds of a short clip is the cheap way to buy those gains. A winning distilled seed does not transfer to `--two-stage` (dev, 30 steps, CFG 3.0), because the two modes take different trajectories (inference). If a clip is good except for a one-second contact moment, `retake --distilled` re-rolls just that segment. It "is not a semantic editor" ([PIPELINES.md](https://github.com/dgrauet/ltx-2-mlx/blob/main/docs/PIPELINES.md)), so treat it as another dice roll on the bad window. Do not count on `--nag` for this. In the repo's own test, **a negative naming distorted hands "did not fix motion-blurred fingers"**, though it did fix a wide-open mouth 4/4 times, at +14% per step ([PIPELINES.md](https://github.com/dgrauet/ltx-2-mlx/blob/main/docs/PIPELINES.md)).

**Lever 5: drive the motion from a real hand with IC-LoRA (highest expected impact, highest effort).** ltx-2-mlx's `ic-lora` subcommand (tier: Stable) runs **LTX-2.3 Union Control (canny, depth, pose)** and **Motion Track Control (sparse BGR spline trajectories)** ([CLAUDE.md](https://github.com/dgrauet/ltx-2-mlx/blob/main/CLAUDE.md); [HF Union Control](https://huggingface.co/Lightricks/LTX-2.3-22b-IC-LoRA-Union-Control)). The verbatim example is `ltx-2-mlx ic-lora -p "a person walking" --lora Lightricks/LTX-2.3-22b-IC-LoRA-Union-Control 1.0 --video-conditioning depth.mp4 1.0 -H 704 -W 1280 -f 97 --frame-rate 24 --low-ram -o out.mp4`. It costs **about 15 min for 1280x704x97 on an M2 Pro 32 GB at q8** ([PIPELINES.md](https://github.com/dgrauet/ltx-2-mlx/blob/main/docs/PIPELINES.md)).

Here is the recipe for a flip. Film your own hand flipping a real patty on a phone, framed like the still. Turn the footage into a canny video with ffmpeg `edgedetect`, which needs no extra model and is the repo's validated identity recipe. Then run `ic-lora` with `--image flux_still.png 0 1.0` so the real clip supplies the contact timing and the still supplies the look ([CLAUDE.md](https://github.com/dgrauet/ltx-2-mlx/blob/main/CLAUDE.md)). `--single-stage` tracks the control most tightly. `--upsample-only --refine-steps N` gives a control-aware refine about 3x faster, which suits drafts ([PIPELINES.md](https://github.com/dgrauet/ltx-2-mlx/blob/main/docs/PIPELINES.md)). Use depth or canny rather than pose, because pose carries the hand skeleton but not the patty (inference). Motion Track suits object-only paths, with tracks drawn by hand or extracted with SpatialTrackerV2 ([HF Motion Track card](https://huggingface.co/Lightricks/LTX-2.3-22b-IC-LoRA-Motion-Track-Control)). Mismatched aspect ratios are center-cropped, and layout conflicts between the control clip and the still will fight. Making the still from frame 0 of your recording avoids that (inference). Three things are unverified: whether `ic-lora` behaves on a **q4** pack (all published timings are q8), what it costs at 704x1280x121, and what `--conditioning-strength` suits hands. Because "re-quantizing loses most of a small LoRA's update," set `LTX2_LORA_MODE=unfused` on q4 ([PIPELINES.md](https://github.com/dgrauet/ltx-2-mlx/blob/main/docs/PIPELINES.md)). This is the same workflow invideo used: phone-filmed blocking used as the motion reference fixed shots that prompting could not ([invideo FAQ](https://invideo.io/faq/why-do-ai-video-models-struggle-with-physical-contact/)).

## When the model cannot do it, edit around it

The most reliable "fix" for a flip is often not generating one. CapCut's guidance on 2026 limits says complex hand and tool actions **"can show temporal anomalies or unnatural morphing,"** that a good take "may not be repeatable," and that you should keep filmed or stock fallbacks for detail shots ([CapCut](https://capcut.com/create/ai-video-limitations-2026)). For shorts-app, that means a shot-grammar rule in the planner (inference). High-risk actions are flips, tosses, knife-through, pour-to-overflow, tearing, cracking and two-hand work. These get split into an approach clip (the spatula hovers or slides under, which is a lowering-type motion you already score well), a 1-2 s cut-on-action insert, and a result clip generated from a separately locked still of the end state (the seared side revealed, steam rising). Low-risk actions are steam, drizzle without hands, slow macro push-ins, cheese pull without hands and reflections. These generate as single clips. Buoyancy and reflection violate physics least often on VideoPhy-2 ([VideoPhy-2](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2503.06800)), which supports that split. Sound then does work the picture cannot. A sharp sizzle spike timed to the cut implies contact. Your FFmpeg SFX stage already allows a separate foley pass, which gives more control than relying on generated audio (inference). For a no-voice cravings channel this costs nothing extra.

## QA can rank takes and catch adherence misses, not judge physics

Automated physics judging is weaker than it looks. On PhyRubric, **zero-shot Qwen3-VL-8B scored Pearson r 0.02 (AUC 0.53) on physical commonsense, which is chance**, though it reached r 0.46 on semantic adherence. Even the 235B model reached only r 0.14 on physics ([PhyRubric](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.13576)). The best fine-tuned open judges reach about r 0.3-0.5: PhyRubric-Judge has physics r 0.32, and VideoScore2 has physical PLCC 52.7 with 39.1% exact accuracy ([VideoScore2](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.22799v1)). Each fine-tuned judge also overfits its own annotation set ([PhyProbe](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.38377)). Physical correctness correlates only weakly with aesthetics, so your existing technical QA (flicker, motion, consistency) will not catch a floating spatula ([VideoPhy-2](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.06800v1)).

So build a cascade. Cheap metrics reject frozen, flickering or morphing clips. A local VLM answers targeted yes/no questions written before generation, such as "Is the spatula under the patty in this frame? Is the seared side visible at the end?" It also ranks the seeds that survive. You make the final pick from the top two. mlx-vlm runs video through `mlx_vlm.generate --video path.mp4 --fps 1.0` for Qwen2.5-VL among others. Qwen3-VL is not yet in its video list ([mlx-vlm](https://github.com/Blaizzy/mlx-vlm)). VideoScore2 is a Qwen2.5-VL-7B fine-tune, which makes an MLX port plausible but untested. One untested heuristic is worth a prototype: run MediaPipe Hand Landmarker, which has a video mode ([MediaPipe](https://ai.google.dev/mediapipe/solutions/vision/hand_landmarker)), across the contact window and flag frames where the hand detection drops out or jumps. Unstable detection on an AI hand is itself an anomaly signal, though nobody has validated it on generated video.

For finishing, a practitioner order is: upscale, correct each clip, apply a LUT at 50-70%, add 35 mm grain at 8-15% opacity at delivery resolution, then a blur under 1 px. Grain added before upscaling gets "stretched into mush" ([invideo](https://invideo.io/blog/luts-film-grain-ai-video/)). This is anecdotal, but it is FFmpeg-native, cheap and reversible, so apply it at assembly rather than to the first frame. The ComfyUI SeedVR2 video upscaler has had full MPS support since v2.5.0. Use FP8 at 12-16 GB, batch_size 4n+1, and watch for the "plastic or highly specular" texture it once produced ([GitHub](https://github.com/numz/ComfyUI-SeedVR2_VideoUpscaler)). Waxy food would undo the realism gain, so A/B it against plain Lanczos plus grain.

## Conclusion

Your bench separates two problems that usually get blurred together. Realism is an image problem that the motion model faithfully preserves, so the largest single gain is in the cheapest stage of the pipeline. Physical contact is a model-class limitation that no current model, open or closed, has solved. The practical goal is therefore not "make LTX understand flipping." It is to give the model less physics to invent: real motion from your own hand, pinned before and after states, shorter clips, more seeds, and edits that hide the instant of contact behind a cut and a sizzle.

That reframing also changes how to read paid models. Seedance-class quality buys better looks and somewhat better odds, not correct physics. The durable advantage for shorts-app is a planner that knows which actions are risky and routes them to the right recipe, plus a review gate that measures adherence, which local judges can do, rather than physics, which they cannot. Once tests A-E produce your own numbers, that routing table becomes a configuration change rather than guesswork.
